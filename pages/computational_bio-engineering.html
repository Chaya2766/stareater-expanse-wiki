Computational bio-engineering

In-software design and simulation of biological systems. Primarily of importance to genetic augmentation, medicine and nanotechnology.

Frameworks for computational bio-engineering tend to be divided by the level at which they abstract the smallest element of the system. Commonly these are:
  1. cellular-scale abstraction
  2. molecular-scale abstraction
Generally, at smaller levels of abstraction more computational power is required, but the behaviour will remain close to reality for a broader range of cases, so for larger projects it is common to run mostly cellular-level simulations alongside far fewer more detailed simulations that verify the findings made that way.

cellular-level abstraction

Cellular-scale models are practically guaranteed to be unable to have edge cases where the simulated biological system behaves wildly differently than the real-world counterpart. They are best used for exploring biological systems that had already been sufficiently studied in reality and through more detailed models, as those are the systems for which sufficient data had already been gathered to build accurate cellular-level abstractions.

In one example, a cellular model focused on human cells, capable of accurately simulating the behaviour of cells with a wide range of genetic makeup, and even interactions between cells with different genetic makeup, requires 1.24e10 bits of information and 6.075e19 bits/s of flip rate on common computronium to simulate a single cell at realtime speed.

It is commonly used to produce sped-up small local simulations of wound healing in reaction to different treatments so that the best one can be applied to the actual patient, or to screen for undesirable interactions between a patient's existing genes and some given biological augmentation they wish to subject themselves to.

It is noted as insufficient to judge the effects of various modifications on the central nervous system, as the abstraction is not capable of recreating neurological activity, which is other times noted as a good thing as it largely circumvents the ethics of full-body simulations. A real-time full-body simulation made with this model typically requires around 4.96e23 bits of memory and 2.43e33 bits/s of flip rate on common computronium.

molecular-level abstraction

Molecular models track every atom in the system and rely on interactions between neighboring atoms, which makes them useful not just for biological applications but also novel chemical compounds with complex behaviours. They are used where cellular models aren't sufficient, and are commonly used as a baseline to which said cellular models are calibrated.

A full-body simulation with molecular precision would require a flip rate somewhere around 2.765e51 bits/s, which is beyond even the theoretical capability of all but the biggest of matrioska brains.

Real use cases rarely go beyond the simulation of a single whole cell or a small group of cells, potentially for uses such as simulating the infection of a cell with a newly found virus or pathogen, studying the process and developing a cure, which itself would likely invoke molecular simulation at the level of just several protein.

Here are some flip rates required for realtime simulation of various biologically relevant objects using a reference molecular simulation system common across the Stareater Expanse:
  • ribosome - 1.103e29 bits/s
  • rhinovirus particle - 3.687e31 bits/s
  • mitochondrion - 3.318e35 bits/s
  • fibroblast cell - 1.106e38 bits/s
  • human egg cell - 1.475e41 bits/s
  • 1ml of biological tissue - 3.687e46 bits/s
The last item on the list is only included as a context reference, as that amount of computation, given hardware that operates right at the landauer limit for room temperature computing, would require roughly the power throughput of a kardashev-2 civilization.
