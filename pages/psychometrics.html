Psychometrics

Psychometrics is the study of measuring and quantifying psychological qualities and capabilities of people and systems. It is used among other things to rate and explain superintelligent systems whose capabilities would otherwise be hard to grasp.

Memory-centric model

The memory-centric model of superintelligence states that greater cognitive capabilities are achieved by simply scaling up the capacity of active memory, and combining it with mental structures that can make better use of that larger workspace. In that view the difference between human-level inteligence and any that lies below or above is just that larger ones are mentally bigger and as such able to contain thoughts in their minds with more complexity at once, allowing them to use more sophisticated mental tools.

Measures and tests of cognitive capability

There are a number of aspects that describe the capability of a given entity's mind. Some can be tested with various methods, while others are more difficult to meaningfully measure or even define. The metrics described below are only intermediate measurements that hint at the cognitive capability of the mind in question, focusing on what can actually be measured. Care was taken to include an example measurement of the human mind in each shown metric if possible as a reference.
  1. Working memory capacity - the number of "things" that a given mind can be actively aware of in one moment, the maximum number of facts the mind can draw a connection between to make an inference, the number of things the mind can keep track of without having to swap any of them to/from long-term memory or external storage (such as written text).
    A standard and easy test for measuring this is to save several numbers in external storage like a sticky note, then remove your own access from that external storage, perform addition of one to each number, write down the resulting incremented numbers and compare them, then repeat the test with more or less numbers until the limit for how many can be reliably processed is found.

    A typical homo terran fails this test when tasked more than 7 numbers, sometimes managing as many as 9 or as few as 5, signifying that an average human's active memory has a capacity of 7 elements (plus or minus 2 from natural variance).

  2. Thought rate - This one in particular is not meaningful in many cases as minds running on computers as software can very often speed themselves up as much as they wish. For an unmodified homo terran one full thought cycle takes a time somewhere in the range of hundreds of miliseconds to complete, with roughly 120 miliseconds being enough to produce the simplest of inferences from new information taken in, which would be equivalent to a thought rate of ≈8.33Hz. One can still specify what thought rate the given entity experiences when it is running at subjective realtime speed, but for some entities which do not experience such a thing as subjective passage of time this too might be a meaningless statement.

    In some cases it can be well correlated to the amount of subjective experience that a given entity is capable of having in a given window of time. An entity whose thoughts take 10 times longer yet produce 10 times more information each can be thought to think no faster or slower, yet the smaller number of thoughts might be indicative of a smaller overall amount of subjective experience. This use of the measurement is often controversial, but proper studies usually go into detail, including self-reported experience of the entity in question, about why such use does or does not apply in a given case.

  3. Information processing rate - A more advanced version of thought rate, generalized to information theory. Information processing rate is typically given in bits or shannons per second (specified as either subjective or real time), though some people take issue with using bits as a unit here. A benefit of this metric over thought rate is that many entities which do not experience time and/or change their thought rate to match the problem are still often limited for one reason or another to some upper bound of information processing rate.

    An average unmodified homo terran brain is thought to be capable of information processing at about 10 bits per subjective second - in information theory terms, 10 bits of information is how much you need to know to narrow down a field of 1024 different possibilities to just one.
Beyond that, here are also many sophisticated statistical tests, which once completed by a given entity produce a single number, or less often a collection of numbers, which can then be usefully statistically correlated to other data. The most known example of this is the "Intelligence Quotient" which contrary to its name does not actually directly measure intelligence, but rather provides a number that can yield valuable insights when plotted against other measures and statistically related to various phenomena. Many similar tests exist, whose results have slightly different statistical relations to other things, which can be combined together to gain greater insights.

A matter of fact modern equivalent of the old IQ test relies on that very idea, that being the the Idala Screenings, which use a number of different score-yielding tests that had been efficiently combined, to produce up to 27 separate indexes that can be statistically correlated to various psychological characteristics, or mapped to a point in a 27-dimensional hypervolume within which these characteristics can be seen as areas that the score can fall into. Simplified 2 or 3 dimensional slices of the full parameter space are usually used for illustrative purposes.
