Inkley Embercold

David Buckley, better known by the online persona Inkley Embercold, was a sci-fi writer having lived in the late 23rd century. He was one of the many victims of the Mitochondrion Inactivating Prion Herpesvirus but his brain was fixed with gluteraldehyde and cryopreserved in an arctic valut after his death due to an insurance agreement he was signed up to.

The brain was recovered and digitized with the appropriate mind uploading techniques several centuries later once the capability to do so became accessible.

Writing

Embercold's writing focussed heavily on extrapolation and exploration of scientific and engineering concepts. He also poured a lot of attention in his writing into parts of the world that are not relevant or relatable to humans, and generally the world beyond the human experience, including characters who experience emotions that humans do not.

He is known for making many predictions about the far future, majority of which turned out to be going roughly in the right direction but missing obvious-in-hindsight caveats, but a few of which had turned out strikingly correct. He had for instance predicted with striking accuracy how unimportant genuine intelligence would turn out to be in industrial applications of "artificial intelligence", portraying a simple combination of basic reasoning and hardcoded skill as vastly more useful than a truly intelligent artificial mind - a prediction that had proven largely correct, but with the real case being a fair bit more complicated and full of edge cases than Embercold's portrayal of it.

Despite the ancient vocabulary some of his stories are still very relatable to people in the modern age. Inkley Embercold's "Yesterday everywhere else" for example follows the daily life of a person in the 13th millenium who lives a unique lifestyle as 7 separate copies of themselves, living in different locations at the same time, uploading their memories to a shared database and undergoing nanotech-aided brain surgery every night to implant them into their brains to maintain a shared identity.

This ancient story is considered an almost 1:1 match for the experience of certain types of digitally reconstructed minds in the 26th century even despite the differences in details (eg. technology to achieve this took centuries rather than thousands of years to develop, and the subjects are massive digital minds living in static infrastructure rather than mobile organic ones) and is sometimes pointed to as a recommended piece of informative writing for humans aspiring to recreate their minds digitally.

Especially passionate readers often recommend trying to parse his writing without auto-translation of the outdated vocabulary, but it is a notably difficult task, especially as Embercold had invented some of his own pieces of vocabulary not used anywhere else at the time, some of which make no sense in the modern context. His use of novel pronouns adopted from even older writing had also contributed to their modern use in the embercold region, especially thanks to his extensive inclusion of a dedicated zie/ziem/zier pronoun in several stories touching the idea of semi-plural entities (despite their strange portrayal, the vocabulary proved uniquely useful in real-world cases much later after the advent of cognitive upscaling which is known to cause identity issues).

Modern day

It is known that Inkley Embercold had his brain fixed with glutaraldehyde and stored in an antarctic vault along with many billions of others. Despite the company having fallen apart within a little over a century, thanks to passive safety design the vault and the brains stored within survived long enough to be taken over by a successor. Loss of documentation had unfortunately caused numerous brains to be of unknown identity, Embercold's being among one of them.

Some time in the 25th century ITON had personally taken on the task of acquiring Embercold's brain and performing a digital reconstruction of it to bring him back to life. What little follow-up information was released suggests that the effort was successful, but that Inkley Embercold did not adapt well to the modern world and had been put under Torpensoor's care. Details of his condition and location had not been extensively released to the public.
