Mitochondrion Inactivating Prion Herpesvirus

Artificial plague responsible for humanity's first gigadeath event, based on the common herpes simplex virus and utilizing specially designed prion protein to delay onset of symptoms, allowing much broader undetected spread.

General description

MIPH is an engineered DNA virus based on the herpes simplex virus. The main payload of MIPH is a protein with two possible folding states, dubbed the "Mitochondrion Inactivating Prion Proteins", shortened as MIPP-α and MIPP-β.

MIPP-α is an inert protein which largely doesn't interact with celular mechanisms or cause any major reactions in most humans. This is the variant of the protein which is produced by cells infected with MIPH.

MIPP-β is a differently folded variant of the protein which has the ability to convert MIPP-α into MIPP-β but is not produced, except for a small probabilistic chance of spontaneous conversion. The function of MIPP-β is similar to cyanide, as it targets the cytochrome c oxidase enzyme in mitochondria, stopping energy production and effectively causing the affected cells to asphyxiate despite presence of oxygen. Once present, MIPP-β is easily spread across cells and through the entire body, especially via the lymphatic system, and can be spread to other humans already containing MIPP-α through bodily fluids, potentially including air-suspended droplets.

The time between initial infection and spontaneous appearance of MIPP-β follows normal distribution with mean time of 5.4Ms (≈ 62.5 days) since infection and 1.2Ms (≈ 14 days) standard deviation, but within just 0.6Ms (≈ 7 days) after initial infection enough MIPP-α is accumulated in the body to be fatal if spontaneous conversion to MIPP-β occurs.

design choices

The choice of Herpesvirus as the basis was likely caused by the herpes simplex being one of the most broadly untreated active diseases in humans, which provided it as a good basis for broad unnoticed spread. The existing behaviour of going dormant for long times in the host with occasional flare-ups also enabled persistence in the host, allowing the pre-symptomatic infection time to be greatly extended.

The two-state protein targeting mitochondria was the most sophisticated part of the virus and a core cause of its long presymptomatic period. Given the tools of the time, this was a masterpiece of bioengineering but not outside of what publicly available tools would have been able to achieve, the most likely tools ironically originating primarily from crowd-sourced projects seeking to develop better treatments to prion diseases. Given that the virus was developed in secret even despite public services having safeguards in place to stall and report such uses, the protein design must have occurred with assistance of open source protein simulation software, advanced expert systems ran entirely on private hardware and would have been conducted by a very dedicated coordinator, but would not necessarily have required a multi-person team.

The opinions of most experts after the case could be summarized as: "the tools had been out there for decades, the only missing piece was someone with the desire and dedication to actually do this".

Prevention and Treatment

A vaccine to MIPH is available, with vast majority of humans experiencing no noticeable symptoms from vaccination. Infection with MIPH in a vaccinated person will result in a small amount of MIPP-α buildup which will gradually be cleared out of the body, but some cells may locally continue producing the protein after incorporating the DNA of the virus, which does not cause concentrations significant enough to be dangerous. In very rare cases, MIPP-α may by simple probability transition to MIPP-β even in a vaccinated person, which primarily kills the cells that had been producing the protein without significantly affecting the rest of the body.

In an unvaccinated person, the first part of treatment is administering the vaccine, which will quickly cause the body to react to the present virus and stop the buildup of MIPP-α, but a potentially fatal concentration may have already been accumulated by that point. Clearing out the protein is usually done by simple dialysis to bring it back down to safe levels. Depending on how advanced the infection was, the patient may be unable to naturally sustain safely low levels of MIPP-α and require routine dialysis treatments until sufficient numbers of cells lose the ability to produce the protein.

There are two common pathways to treatment of cases that advanced past the accumulation of MIPP-α to dangerous levels:
1. Introduce MIPP-β into the patient's body while under constant dialysis, causing it to kill primarily the cells producing MIPP-α. This is only a viable option if the patient's body is only infected in small fraction, and especially if the brain had not been infiltrated by the virus.
2. Perform gene therapy which induces the patient's body to produce an enzymes that break down MIPP-α and MIPP-β, so that the continued protein production cannot accumulate to dangerous levels.
