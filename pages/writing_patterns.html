Writing patterns for Stareater Expanse

This page is about how I write the Stareater Expanse.

I will explain here what my goal is and why I write certain things the way I do. That might also be useful for knowing how to read/interpret some of the things I write.

Due to my observation that most people, myself included, say a lot of things they don't actually do, I'll allow myself to write an ideal set of guidelines that in theory I would like to follow in my own writing, and then just add a section that goes over how well I had followed these guidelines, and how I had broken them in practice.

This page also includes links to some of the software I use in my writing, for anyone who might find it helpful.

Foundation of the world

The goal of Stareater Expanse as a setting is to explore available technology and things that can be done within the bounds of real laws of physics. The particular way to do this I had chosen involves verifying what things are possible using exact calculation. This, together with a few other wishes I have for what my writing should be, leads to a few general guidelines that describe the writing well:

1. mathematical modeling is the basis of everything. Everything stems from some mathematical model, or a conclusion of it, and the only reason that takes priority above following the mathematical conclusion of the model, is if the model does not logically apply to the situation. The findings are to be accepted no matter how awkward, inconvenient, overpowered or disappointing the results are. Things should also be modeled based on available data rather than mere guessing - some amount of guessing is acceptable but if doing so, try to underestimate the gains and overestimate difficulties, so that if the truth turns out to be different than the guess then it more likely lifts constraints rather than adds disappointment.

2. there is no intended balance. This touches on the previous point, but when I introduce new technologies I will make them as powerful as the math says and I will force the world to adapt instead of making the new addition weaker just so that it fits in. As such, I am never "balancing" anything - the balance is meant to arise naturally (and surprisingly, it often does) without explicit tweaking. If something is unacceptably bad, look for alternatives and put them through the mathematical rigor until one of them turns out to be good enough instead of artificially strengthening it. If something is too powerful, force the world to adapt instead of artificially weakening it.

3. Don't try to fill all the available space, only take as much as you need.
There might be an urge to try to fill all physical space available in the solar system by writing grand nations and describing broad societal patterns, maybe even planetary governments - reject it, try to put these ideas in small things. The amount of space that a single habitat provides is already massive, you can fit a lot of content there, this is even more true for a planet, and as far as the solar system is concerned it may as well be practically infinite. This does not lessen diversity, but makes sure that space is available for more interesting content, and provides a very satisfying sense of scale that shows off just how big everything actually is.
The point is to reflect just how much space there is by filling it only as necessary - if you want to write a planetary empire, see if you can try to write it as a single habitat instead, and if you find that you can't, try just a few habitats, trust me you'll find that the sheer amount of space available allows for it. The second but related understanding is to not write filler content - write things that can be looked closely at, places that could actually be visited, people that could be talked to, not empty territories whose only purpose is to be someone's or cultures who don't have an example member to show. This comes together to present a society concentrated in small points among vast swaths of empty space, yet nonetheless with points so mindnumbingly complex that you could get lost within them - in this way somehow better than in other ways the sense of scale is conveyed in great detail.

4. No filler content. If something is being written about, make it meaningful. Make places which can be visited, characters who can be talked to, tools that can actually be used. When a reader looks at something, they should know that that it is a real place - this reinforces the feeling of scale that one gets when looking at cosmic-scale society. Bringing in a game creation metaphore, nothing should be just an unreachable graphic on the skybox, everything that is shown should be modeled and interactable.

Where I fail to meet these ideals, and what problems they face in practice

Guideline 1 had been followed only where it matters - there's a lot of content such as the personalities of my characters and other minor non-technical details where it is just not applicable. With this being the main challenge I can't honestly say anything more than that I'm doing my best, and I think I'm doing quite well.
Guideline 2 had been upheld very well at least in my self-assessment.
Guideline 3 had been broken many times. Sometimes I realize that I did it, then go back and reflect on what I've written, and try to pull the scope back to the minimum I actually need.
Guideline 4 is somewhat questionable. The lack of filler content makes the setting feel empty, a little bit of filler content might actually help as long as I keep it somewhat full of meaningful content.

Advice to myself for when I'll end up looking at this page again:
  1. When a page mentions some general trend or describes a cultural movement, try to explicitly link an example character or habitat to show alignment with guideline 4. This can be done by putting a card at the bottom and making the name of the card a popup tip which explains why the card is there. It will probably also stop you from forgetting that you did write an example of something.

Supporting software

I picked as much of my software as I could so that everything would be stored in text format, and as such easy to version using git or make manual edits to. Additionally all the software can be downloaded through apt if you're using linux. None of these tools require internet connection to work once you have them downloaded.
  1. Documents with mathematical notation: LaTeX
    packages: texlive texlive-latex-extra texstudio
    LaTeX is effectively a programming language which compiles into PDF documents.
    Texstudio is a very helpful IDE for LaTeX which I use to write the documents.
  2. Offline git tracking software: Gitg
    packages: gitg
    This makes it way easier to make and use offline git repositories since I'm not good at using the command line git tool and having a graphical UI really helps. This is also the reason why I am so insistent on text files for everything, ever since this app made using git easy for me I had been keeping track of all my important writing this way.
  3. 3D modeling: OpenSCAD
    packages: openscad
    This is effectively a programming language which compiles into 3D models. It can be used normally as an application, but a very helpful other property of it is that it can be ran in terminal as a command with flags telling it exactly what camera position and angle to use and such to produce renders of the 3d models.
    It also has the amazing property of rendering images with no dithering, meaning that it is by default compatible with my artstyle which largely prohibits any use of smoothing or blur.
  4. Logic circuits: Logisim
    packages: logisim
    Logical circuit simulator and editor. I so far had only used it in one document so it is not really important, but it stores circuits in text form so I felt it worth mentioning. As of writing this, there is an updated version called "Logisim Evolution" but I am still using the old one just because it is available on apt.
  5. Doing all the math: Qalculate
    packages: qalculate-gtk
    The truly special thing about this calculator is that I can type in things like (1361W/m^2)*(20cm*30cm)*20% or (1kg/L)*(5m^3) and it just handles the units for me.
    Massive improvement over the old days where I used a calculator that only does numbers and had to convert the units manually.
    Don't get me wrong, developing the skill of manually converting units to the right form is great, but once you've learned how to do that it just becomes a massive waste of time, and you will always be more likely to make a mistake converting units manually than just typing them into the calculator along with the numbers and having it do that for you, with the benefit of sometimes being hit with volume expressed in kilograms per square meter and other such things which immediately lets you know that you screwed something up instead of having to painfully discover that mistake later after you've already made numerous follow-up calculations.
