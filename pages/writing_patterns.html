Writing patterns (meta information page)

This page is about how I write the Stareater Expanse.

I will explain here what my goal is and why I write certain things the way I do. That might also be useful for knowing how to read/interpret some of the things I write.

I'll do my best to write here only what my writing actually is rather than what I wish it was.

core values

1. physical realism first: between interesting characters, captivating storylines, physically realistic technology and events, and a satisfying sense of balance, I above all prioritize physically realistic technology and events. Stareater Expanse is closer to a physics sandbox in the form of writing than a story, I generally ask what would actually happen and stick with the conclusions even if they might seem disappointing to most people. The inaccuracies that there are generally stem from me getting tunnel-vision on a certain solution, or thinking something sounds plausible enough and leaving it for future me to solve down to the mathematical detail just to find out I can't actually make it work, or making specific numerical assumptions that I deem "close enough" without realizing they are actually really far off (I am only human >w<; takes a lot of time to check things). These are also all reasons for why my writing is prone to huge rewrites, as I am willing to throw away quite a lot of writing just to fix an underlying mistake.

2. be exact: when I describe something in detail I'd like the reader to be able to grab the calculator, do the math (even though most won't, because I don't give homework to my readers), and see that it actually is mathematically consistent, or alternatively find out that it is not and make their own conclusions, but not just have to trust my word due to being unable to know how I came to the conclusions I made. Even when I'm talking about things that are inherently not mathematical, like new emotions that humans cannot experience, I try to convey a mental model that is consistent and detailed.

potentially strange choices

  1. across thousands of years, and even with availability of artificial superintelligence, physics knowledge progressed nearly nothing past what we know today. I am essentially assuming that humanity solved physics in the modern day (which I agree is totally unrealistic, seeing that as of writing, I am constantly hearing of physicists having discovered some new thing) because it lets me justify sticking to the equations and things I can know for reasonably sure, as opposed to assuming what physics developments there would be in the future and coming up with new laws of physics I assumed humanity will discover which are practically guaranteed to be wrong. A third option would have been to skillfully use vagueness to disguise the ridiculousness of whatever new discoveries I decide to portray in my writing but, and make very hard-thought assumptions for certain physical problems such that they result in interesting conclusions - as cool as such writing could be, that's not my goal, so I'll point you to Orion's Arm Universe Project instead for such writing, as I think they did a wonderful job balancing realism with assumptions about future developments.

Supporting software

I picked as much of my software as I could so that everything would be stored in text format, and as such easy to version using git or make manual edits to. Additionally all the software can be downloaded through apt if you're using linux. None of these tools require internet connection to work once you have them downloaded.
  1. Documents with mathematical notation: LaTeX
    packages: texlive texlive-latex-extra texstudio
    LaTeX is effectively a programming language which compiles into PDF documents.
    Texstudio is a very helpful IDE for LaTeX which I use to write the documents.
  2. Offline git tracking software: Gitg
    packages: gitg
    This makes it way easier to make and use offline git repositories since I'm not good at using the command line git tool and having a graphical UI really helps. This is also the reason why I am so insistent on text files for everything, ever since this app made using git easy for me I had been keeping track of all my important writing this way.
  3. 3D modeling: OpenSCAD
    packages: openscad
    This is effectively a programming language which compiles into 3D models. It can be used normally as an application, but a very helpful other property of it is that it can be ran in terminal as a command with flags telling it exactly what camera position and angle to use and such to produce renders of the 3d models.
    It also has the amazing property of rendering images with no dithering, meaning that it is by default compatible with my artstyle which largely prohibits any use of smoothing or blur.
  4. Logic circuits: Logisim
    packages: logisim
    Logical circuit simulator and editor. I so far had only used it in one document so it is not really important, but it stores circuits in text form so I felt it worth mentioning. As of writing this, there is an updated version called "Logisim Evolution" but I am still using the old one just because it is available on apt.
  5. Doing all the math: Qalculate
    packages: qalculate-gtk
    The truly special thing about this calculator is that I can type in things like (1361W/m^2)*(20cm*30cm)*20% or (1kg/L)*(5m^3) and it just handles the units for me.
    Massive improvement over the old days where I used a calculator that only does numbers and had to convert the units manually.
    Don't get me wrong, developing the skill of manually converting units to the right form is great, but once you've learned how to do that it just becomes a massive waste of time, and you will always be more likely to make a mistake converting units manually than just typing them into the calculator along with the numbers and having it do that for you, with the benefit of sometimes being hit with volume expressed in kilograms per square meter and other such things which immediately lets you know that you screwed something up instead of having to painfully discover that mistake later after you've already made numerous follow-up calculations.
