Venus

Venus, originally a hot planet with extremely thick carbon dioxide atmosphere, had been turned into a somewhat livable frozen planet across roughly 200 years in a terraformation project that never reached completion.

A sun shade at its L1 point with the sun keeps the planet cold, while the orbital space around it still benefits from nearly twice as much solar energy as at earth's distance.

Currently the planet is active and thriving despite the incomplete terraformation, with many people living on its surface in the extreme cold and unbreathable atmosphere.

Venusian sky

Due to its current predicament of being covered in dry ice, Venus has an entire light economy which makes sure that the average surface illumination stays around 77 watts per square metre, corresponding to 7238 lux, roughly the level of illumination commonly experienced on earth from a mostly clear sky in the moments when the sun is blocked out by a cloud. This exact level of solar irradiance is needed to maintain the temperature of 192 kelvin, an awkward compromise between keeping the surface livable at least to some people while also keeping the atmospheric carbon dioxide frozen.

Instead of a single large mirror, orbiting the planet once every 24 hours, simulating the terran day-night cycle, a swarm of small mirror satelites orbits the planet. These appear in the sky like thousands of bright stars, much brighter than any real star, most of them as bright as the full moon viewed from earth. Surface settlements often request more or less light at any given time to be reflected onto them, and neighbouring communities negotiate to make the best use of the available sunlight where some of them follow completely custom day-night cycles that do not approximate earth's day and night.

Any of the unused sunlight is evenly distributed across the surface of venus, keeping it from getting too cold while also not warming it up so much that the dry ice would begin to re-sublimate, or swapped out for an equal wattage of beamed microwave power as needed. This arrangement is forseen to continue for at least as long as it takes to remove all the excess and frozen carbon dioxide from the surface of venus, which is an even more extreme effort than the original proposal of covering it with insulation and burying it under added oceans and soil, and will take a very long time to complete.

How the venus terraformation project was killed

The project closely followed the plan of "terraforming venus quickly" originally described by Paul Birch in 1991, it is sometimes poetically said that's the real date on which the project began. Venus terraformation officially began in [year?] with the approval of solar shade construction at the Venus-Sun L1 point.

Initial stages

A massive solar shade 15.4E+6m in diameter was to be placed at the Sun-Venus L1 point, roughly 2.5E+8 metres from the planet. Within [?] years the shade had been completed, made from material delivered to the L1 point via hohmann transfer from the asteroid belt. With the heat of the sun blocked, Venus naturally began to radiate away its heat.

65 years after the sun was blocked out, liquid carbon dioxide began to rain down onto Venus and forming oceans on its surface. At 145 years in, the surface had reached zero celsius, with the remaining atmosphere being 26 bar of still mostly carbon dioxide. At 180 years in, "dry snow" began to fall as the atmosphere cooled down enough for the carbon dioxide to solidify under the high pressure atmosphere. Finally at 200 years since the sun was blocked out the "stage 3" was complete - most of the carbon dioxide had been frozen out of the atmosphere and laid on the surface, deposited in vast oceans covered by ice and snow. At that point the surface had a temperature of -56 Celsius, and a pressure close to 7 atmospheres, which would slowly continue to fall onto the surface as "dry snow" over the following 10 years, until all of the carbon dioxide fell out of the atmosphere, leaving just 2.8 atmospheres of nitrogen at -81C.

Premature inhabitation

Within the period between 200 and 210 years into the project, there was a huge rise in surface population of venus. There were many people who wanted to experience the semi-terraformed planet in person. The point where the surface pressure of Venus dropped low enough to no longer necessitate special breathing gas mixtures of helium and oxygen was the last straw for many who for one reason on another wanted to settle the surface.

Disputes between the organization in charge of Venus terraformation and the settlers, not wanting to give up their land to let the project proceed with sealing the frozen carbon dioxide under a new surface, effectively softlocked the project. In some of the settlements, new life was being engineered, more fit for the environment of frozen Venus, including genetically engineered humans more resilient to the extreme cold and capable of becoming frozen without sustaining fatal damage, those would go on to become known as the homo borealis much later.

It was increasingly clear that many of the settlers were not intending to leave, even if they originally meant their stay to be temporary. The truth in many cases was simply that they already were venusians for over a hundred years by that point, and for one reason or another, be it because a new generation disagreed with the previous, or the original supporters of the terraformation project changed their mind, simply no longer wanted any more change to their planet that they would be forced to adapt to again, especially as the further developments were to include dropping icy bodies into the atmosphere to add water to the planet.

Hostilities on Venus

Third parties had become aggressive towards the venusian settlers around 212 years into the project after news of the disputes over land use became more widely reported on. A common lens through which the situation was seen, was that of newcommers settling the planet and interfering with the terraformation efforts, not that of long-term inhabitants no longer agreeing with the project. Some ad-hoc militias formed by new arrivals to the planet led months-long campaigns in the frozen terrain, some attempting to desettle or simply kill off the inhabitants so that the terraformation could continue.

These would be widely reported on, and no doubt led to attrocities that lit hearts ablaze across venus and beyond, but in broader perspective they were small, and the life of an average venusian remained untouched by this. Venusians themselves even often viewed these as just horrible things happening in other countries, as communities scattered across the planet did not view themselves as part of one whole, though coverage from offworld commenters almost universally missed the nuance of venusian geopolitics.

These isolated efforts eventually fizzled out, though they would be remembered as a uniquely violent time in the history of venus, a time when for once the danger came from other people and not just the environment itself.

Status quo

Functioning in the frozen environment proved not too difficult both for people and industry. Homo borealis as they came to be known had matured into a well defined artificially engineered species in the human genus and continue to thrive across venus. A few memorials still stand to those killed in the offworlder terrorist attacks.

In the end despite the project not reaching its goal in full, it still left venus it in a vastly more habitable state than before. The average human requires only warm clothing and an air supply to survive the surface, some even opting for natural fur instead.

The carbon dioxide snow so abundant on the surface proves quite a good source of oxygen when the energy to split it is available, and the extreme cold of venus provides a uniquely fitting environment for use of Lanthanium Heptahydrate and Hydrogen Sulfide, which when made into highly internally presurized wires[how?] are actually superconducting at venus' normal outdoors temperature.
