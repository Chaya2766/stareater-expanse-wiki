Steganographic social networks

Steganographic social networks are second layer social networks which secretly use the more open social networks and more broadly other types of information as their medium. They operate by embedding hidden messages within openly visible things, most often text, images, audio or binary files. A message can be hidden within another message such that only those with a special key can tell it apart from natural noise and read it. These networks typically rely on unknowing participants sharing the plainly visible message in which the hidden message is embedded, and/or their other users re-encoding the hidden message into a completely different public message or even a completely different format while preserving the hidden data.

Steganographic social networks enable communication that cannot be detected by outside parties, as such they are commonly used by the criminal and the oppressed alike, as well as those who simply don't want others to prod at their secrets (which to be fair are largely all the same thing). Exchanging public keys to be used for such communication before parting ways with your loved ones can help you keep them safe under some circumstances, but is also illegal in some areas. Mutually trusted parties with membership to an organization can also use these types of schemes to recognize each other and coordinate without displaying any signs that a non-member could detect. Unknowing participants can also get caught in the crossfire of investigations by sharing messages containing data they could not have possibly known about.

Hidden message mediums

Here are just a few examples of the ways that additional messages get invisibly encoded into public content:
  1. Encoding scheme which converts the message into a series of algorithmically made natural-looking typos across the length of a large text article or numerous, potentially even thousands of shorter chronologically ordered text posts.
  2. Encoding the message into the least significant colour bit of pixels in an image, mimicking the natural noise of camera sensors.
  3. Encoding the message into the least significant bit of audio samples in a recording or music file.
  4. Acquiring a vocal implant capable of subtly modulating the tone of your voice in an unrelated conversation to encode digital data.
  5. Arranging items in a physical environment such that when analyzed with the right decoding scheme their arrangement could be interpreted as unambiguous digital data, which could also include their arrangement in the temporal dimension, ie. specific timing of events.
Truly sophisticated steganographic communication schemes can be completely indistinguishable from noise to outsiders, but history is full of schemes that were thought to be unbreakable up until someone did find a way to detect them, and some of those are even still used in the present, as even though the tools to detect them exist, it is still so difficult that they are deemed "safe enough" by the people that use them.
As of the year 3000 these schemes are so sophisticated and all-encompassing that if you truly wanted to not just mitigate them but actually completely eliminate them you would have to contend with the fact that, if you look far enough, merely existing and being observable is sufficient for a suspicion that you are trying to secretly communicate something, and if you think even harder you could even come up with circumstances where not existing could be interpreted as such.

Proof of Work "boosting"

There is also a trend of using proof of work to convey the importance of a message, which usually means that when someone makes a post whose text has an unnaturally low hash number it is likely to be passed on further. This behaviour had spilled over into some of the openly visible social networks where various other social behaviours had attached themselves to this mechanism. Among others there is a vague common belief that a message that carries the mark of massive computational power is also more trustworthy, or at the very least important, thus it is often used for "boosting" your posts so that they are more likely to be shared. This text for example has an unnaturally small SHA256 hash, though that doesn't on its own say anything about its trustworthiness. Some users of the networks where this became habitual had also taken to applying proof of work to their username as a way of showing off how much computational power they have access to. These networks then, mostly unknowingly but some no doubt knowingly too, had become great mediums for carrying hidden messages.-4580897539

In the context of steganographic social networks this has an additional function of clarifying that given piece of data was intentionally constructed, as in some schemes it is possible to encounter false positives, ie. your key detected and decoded a hidden message where in reality there was none and you are left trying to look for meaning in actual random noise. It is incredibly unlikely (on the order of 1 in 1 048 576) that if you put such random noise through a hash function you will get 20 leading zero bits, or 5 leading zeros in hexadecimal encoding.
