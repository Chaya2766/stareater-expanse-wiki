InterPlanetary Positioning And Communication System

IPPACS is a self-configuring network that enables position-finding, communication and to a limited extent also timekeeping.

The self-configuring nature of the network makes it a common tool among explorers going where nobody provides services, be it distant space, underground, underwater or through wreckage of formerly functional infrastructure, but there is little argument that it could rival actual intelligently laid out and configured, dedicated positioning and communication systems in terms of accuracy and bandwidth.

I am strongly inspired by the InterPlanetary File System as well as the Bitmessage protocol.

Location and Timekeeping

Since light takes time to travel, moving away from a clock inherently causes it to appear to be late, which forms the basis of how IPPACS nodes locate themselves. If you know that a distant clock is precisely synchronized to your own, you can calculate the distance based on this delay. If you see at least 3 clocks at different locations which are synchronized with both each other and with your clock, you can triangulate your own position in relation to them.

The time reported by the nodes is not meant to be accurate in absolute terms, but rather it is meant to simply be close enough that the nodes are able to communicate with one another. A way to think about it is that if two nodes are one lightsecond apart, they will assume their own clock should be one second ahead of the other node's clock. If there is a discrepancy, the nodes will subtly slow down or speed up their own clock to minimize it. An "absolute current time" would have been meaningless anyway given that the network is spread across a range of different gravitational potentials, thereby causing different parts of it to drift not just due to inaccuracy but also due to genuinely experiencing a different rate of time passage.

Because of this, the date reported by the network drifts around as the nodes try to stay in sync with each other, but the sheer mass of the network generally keeps it from becoming massively inaccurate. A lot of nodes also rely on the universal coordinated time provided by the various standard-keeping agencies of the solar system as a source of truth, and as such tend to gently pull the rest of the network back to what is broadly considered the "correct" time.

A trick borrowed by the GPS system that IPPACS also uses, is that whenever a node broadcasts its time, location, dilation and other information necessary to derive your own location and time relative to it, it also includes a packet of data specifying the location of one other randomly picked nearby IPPACS node that it knows about, allowing you to gradually gather connections to other nodes and build a picture of the whole system.

Even though the system is specifically referred to as "interplanetary", there is nothing stopping the system from being expanded across numerous star systems, especially in case of 2 star systems with present and overlapping oort clouds. The system will adapt just fine and can even handle being split into separate networks for long times before being joined back together. The critical requirement is that the networks being joined must have originally been part of the same network, or at least initiated with similar data, or else they might simply think all of the nodes making up the other network are malfunctioning and refuse to synchronize. In general the time drift between IPPACS networks across different systems in the spider cluster is small enough that low-bandwidth interstellar laser communication is enough to keep the networks synchronized, much helped by the presence of pulsars within the cluster itself.

Bootstraping

A map of the solar system, including the gravitational potentials, is baked into IPPACS software, allowing these hints to be traced back to actual satellite locations by just simulating the trajectory and time dilation based on the received data.

communication protocols

IPPACS packages all data into file-like containers addressed by either the hash of their contents, or a public key and the fitting cryptographic signatures. This is mostly a result of the system having originally been designed exclusively for file sharing and not realtime communication, and having ended up used in this way merely as a result of faster and faster computers allowing the computational overhead to become unnoticeable to people who took note of the extreme reliability of the system and decided to use it for realtime communication.

The hash of a file is entirely determined by the bits that make it up, and cannot be easily back-calculated other than by brute force. Creating two files with an identical hash in a reasonable time by simple brute force methods would require vastly more computing power than can be attained within the observable universe, so when a file is requested from the network by a hash, it is practically guaranteed that anyone, anywhere, anytime, who requests that file, will get the exact identical file (as long as it is available anywhere in the network).

The cryptographic signature is determined by the hash as well as by the key. Conventionally, a file can be identified by a public key, in which case, requesting a file for that public key will yield the most recently signed file that has a signature valid for that key. Forging a cryptographic signature is similarly not easy, and would take vastly more computing power than the observable universe can provide, but the issue of verifying authenticity is more nuanced due to the possibility of signing keys being stolen. Although the software itself is technically free of exploits, one still has to consider the possibility of user error.

Whether a file is shared or requested, it has to be paired with a "proof of work", which is just a string of bits that, when added to the file, result in a much smaller hash value. The real value of the proof of work is weighed against the age of the file, so that a newer file with the same proof of work value has higher priority to be kept in the node's cache. This naturally results in more important files being more available, since when many devices are requesting a given file they will naturally end up updating its proof of work such that it is more likely to stay available in the nearby nodes. It also prevents people from clogging the network by flooding it with requests, as file requests with low proof of work will be given less priority and eventually be discarded by nodes when space is needed for other, newer requests. If the request does reach a node that has the requested file, and by chance it has a higher proof of work than that which is already attached to the file, it will become the new proof of work, thereby increasing the priority of that file.

In both cases, nodes that downloaded a given file, whether for their own use or because they were used as an intermediate between other nodes, will store a copy of that file if it has the storage space to do so. This means the network won't be crippled by someone repeatedly requesting large files, since the data will only have to traverse the network once before the closest nodes have their own copies. It also increases the availability of files where they are most relevant.

The priority of which files get transferred first is decided by their size as well as the attached proof of work - the smaller the file the higher priority it has, and also the bigger the attached proof of work the higher priority the file has. In practice this means that if someone wants a file to propagate across the network faster, they can easily do so at the cost of spending extra computing power. This creates competition in areas where demand for bandwidth is much larger than the available network througput, as people will perform more computation to ensure their most important messages are transferred first.

bandwidth with different communication media

bits per second that can be achieved with commonly available hardware and the IPPACS protocols at various distances.
  • Fiber-optic connection: 4e14 bits/s (50TB/s) per fiber at up to 50km distance.
  • short-range Laser communication: 2e11 bits/s (25GB/s) per discernable beam at ranges up to 1000km. Potentially enables surface-orbit communication but presence of a dense atmosphere or certain atmospheric conditions might make it ineffective.
  • long-range Laser communication: 8e6 bits/s (1MB/s) per discernable beam at ranges around 2.25e11 metres (≈ 750 lightseconds). The "discernable" requirement becomes significant here as even a swarm of satelites around an entire planet might be too close together to appear as separate beam sources at such huge distances.
  • short-range Radio communication: 1e10 bits/s (1.25GB/s) at ranges around 10 metres. Bandwidth decreases with each device trying to communicate in the same area at the same time, since it is difficult to isolate radio signals at such distances.
  • long-range Radio communication: 2e8 bits/s (25MB/s) at ranges around 10km, 2e7bits/s (2.5MB/s) at 100km, 2e6 bits/s (250kB/s) at 1000km. Potentially good surface-orbit communication option when laser comms not available, as it is much less vulnerable to being blocked by bad atmospheric conditions.
  • electrical conductor communication: 1e9 bits/s (125MB/s) at or below 1km. The electrical conductor in question could also be carrying useful quantities of DC or low frequency AC electricity at the same time as the signal without interfering with the data transmission, so IPPACS can be easily integrated into power grids as a method of communication between power grid devices. The bandwidth will be divided between all the devices attempting to use the same continuous conductor for communication.
  • sonic data transmission: 2e5 bits/s (25kB/s) at 1km in water. Bandwidth will be divided between devices trying to communicate since sonic data transmission is so notorious for interfering with one another.

File sharing

Files on the IPPACS network are identified by 512-bit UIDs, typically encoded in base62 like for example "vx2xcED34puKC9ZUny1cWyAa8FLSVDtOivehsIhjtJR7Onlw2Wvc1neFEmeSONFUyArjApWpteJC0i6W5SG8KF".

The UID might either directly be a hash of the file, in which case that file is retrieved from any node which knows it, or it might be a hash of a descriptor containing a public key, in which case the swarm will attempt to return the newest file signed with that key.
