Finespun standard environment


Together with the specification, there is a set of custom units for Finespun, used primarily as a thought experiment, and very occasionally applied in casual use, but discouraged for any serious engineering uses in favor of just using SI units. For the sake of common daily use, ox and °W do prove much more convenient than atm and °C.

Math document is available here.

Environment specifications

Atmosphere: 21 kilopascals absolute pressure, 100% oxygen.
Temperature: 290 kelvin absolute temperature.
Gravity: 2m/s2 centrifugal acceleration.

This environment provides the exact required amount of oxygen for a human to breathe, but none of the other atmospheric gasses.
The less dense atmosphere means greatly diminished role of convection in heat transfer, as such heat transfer by radiation and conduction dominates.
Water evaporates much more easily in such low pressure, which greatly enhances drying and may cause discomfort to baseline humans, requiring increased water intake to offset the quicker moisture loss.

Water in this environment boils when heated to 333K, 60°C, 140°F, 100°W.

Custom units

Oxybar

Oxybar, shortened ox, unit of pressure. 1 ox is equal to 0.21 bar or 21kPa.

It is the pressure at which an atmosphere of pure oxygen is sufficient for human breathing, and also the partial pressure of oxygen in standard terran air. Terran air contains 20.95% oxygen by molar fraction, as such 1 bar of terran air contains very close to 1 ox of oxygen partial pressure.
There are records dating back to the 21st century of gregorian epoch of humans surviving for 2 years in an environment with only 0.475 bar of absolute pressure [wikipedia] which translates to 0.474 ox of oxygen partial pressure, as such 0.5 ox is considered a rule of thumb limit for permanent habitability.
At 0.381 ox of oxygen partial pressure a baseline human will have difficulty sleeping, and digesting food is extremely difficult, as such 0.4 ox is considered a rule of thumb limit for temporary habitability.
The absolute pressure in earth's "climber's death zone" is 0.356 bar, meaning 0.355 ox of oxygen partial pressure - at or below that oxygen pressure no amount of acclimatization can allow a baseline human to survive.
At 0.298 ox absolute pressure (6.25kPa) the boiling point of water drops enough to be boiled by human body temperature [wikipedia], as such 0.3 ox is considered a rule of thumb limit for short exposure survivability.

At a pressure of ox (= bar) water would boil at 333.15 kelvin (333.15 °C, 333.15 °F, 333.15 °W)

Waterboil (placeholder name)

0°W is equal to 273.15K, the temperature at which water freezes under 1 ox of pressure.
100°W is equal to 333.15K, the temperature at which water boils under 1 ox of pressure.
Converting between °C and °W is easily done by just multiplying or dividing by 0.6.
28°W is considered room temperature on Finespun.
35°W is considered room temperature (21°C) according to chemical standard conditions.
63°W is human body temperature.
75°W is the lowest temperature known to cause burns to human skin over very long exposure times.
82°W water will cause third degree burns in 300 seconds, 90°W in 30 seconds, 100°W in 5 seconds.
105°W is the minimum recommended safe cooking temperature for pork, 120°W for beef, and 124°W for poultry. Since all of these are above the boiling point, the meat will first dry out completely before it can reach the recommended temperature.
Absolute zero is at -455.25°W, but the custom unit loses its usefulness that far outside of its 0 to 100 range.

Cooking

Common recommendations for temperatures to cook meat safely call for temperatures between 63°C (105°W) and 72°C (120°W), which means that meat cannot be safely cooked in this environment, except by cooking it to the point where all the water had evaporated and then some more, or cooking it in a pressurized container.
If meat is cooked to the correct temperature in this environment without extra pressure, it will first have to evaporate away all of its water, turning it into jerky.

Falling

An object falling from metres in a gravity of m/s2 will take 1 seconds to fall and hit the ground at 2 m/s.
For daily purposes this means that in 2m/s2 a fall only becomes dangerous to a typical baseline human above roughly 6.25 metres, and the average human jump velocity of 2.82m/s translates to 2 metres of jump height and 2.82s of airtime.

A well-trained baseline might be able to "tuck and roll" to avoid damage from falls as large as 25 metres.
