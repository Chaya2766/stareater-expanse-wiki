Cognitive upscaling

Cognitive Upscaling refers to the process of improving mental capabilities of an intelligent or otherwise thinking system by making large, significant changes to its structure, most often by adding new parts to the brain or multiplying and rearranging existing ones, and with little to no use of classical compute-heavy machine learning processes. The system in question could be an AI, a digitized human mind, or some combination of both. As digital minds can operate extremely quickly, cognitive upscaling can appear to be done very quickly to outside observers if sufficient computing power is present, though in many cases it happens over very long times as a result of limited computing power availability forcing the system to run at slower speed.

The core enabling element of cognitive upscaling is the access to many pretrained AI models, as combining existing neural networks is vastly less computationally expensive than training new ones. As such, the result of cognitive upscaling is typically a composite mind.

Performing cognitive upscaling

Successfully performing cognitive upscaling usually involves a framework that includes a versioning system and a library of pretrained neural networks with known function. The person, system or otherwise mind undergoing cognitive upscaling will save versioned states of themselves and then make changes to their brain before testing the results. If they deem the change to be beneficial or otherwise successful, they will make another versioned state, and continue to make further changes. If the change is not successful, the mind is reverted to the latest saved state - this may or may not cause the loss of memories, which from one side some may find scary and equivalent to death, while from the more practical side in such a case it is necessary to have some way to know that such a change was made and that it was unsuccesful, as to not get stuck in a loop of making that same change over and over again.

Any attempt at conducting this process will usually end up producing some changes that are broken and unusable, and somewhat less often may produce changes that themselves are functional but lead to a dead end further down the road. As such the versioning will usually end up producing a tree-like structure with lots of branches as well as a core trunk of sorts that is the path from the original state to the version which was accepted as final. Storing all the versions can require massive amounts of data storage even with an optimized versioning scheme, and while some keep working on their original version tree, most opt to trim off data from failed versions or even discard the entire tree some time after they reach a point they are satisfied with.


Knowing which version works and which doesn't is also a nontrivial matter, and as such, often automation of that judgement is required. A mind modifying itself may sometimes introduce changes that cripple it to the point it cannot even recognize the fact that it had done damage to itself. It is quite common to use AI agents and standardized tests for this purpose, in an arrangement where each time a version is saved, the mind in question will answer or otherwise perform the tests, and save the results together with the new version, relying on the AI agents to roll it back to previous state in the event that it can no longer function well enough to do that on it's own. Some others also opt to use full copies of their own original mind for that purpose instead of AI agents.

Ofcourse that is all optional, but attempting to make radical improvements to one's own brain in vivo with no backups is ill advised. Failure to prepare this way often leads to what is colloquially known as an intelligence implosion, ie. getting stuck in a broken state with no way back, or emerging from it still functional but less capable than before.

The proven method: fully-connected composite minds

Over time a surprisingly simple model for a basic composite mind had come out of the open source communities that began work on tools for self-guilded cognitive upscaling about as soon as the idea became known. Many different translator networks had already been trained which can act as bridges between two minds - the exact compatibility depends on the particular translator model and the mind in question, some for instance do a good job connecting two human brains together, meanwhile others work well for one particular AI model or a group of AI models, and so on. In discussion these translator networks are usually referred to as bridges.

A number of generalist AI models (referred to as brain nodes) are linked together into a network, where every brain node is linked directly to all other brain nodes with a bridge. This can be enough for very small networks with just 4 or maybe 8 brain nodes, but issues very quickly pile up when moving to numbers higher than that, as two bridges interfacing with the same brain node can interfere with each other if they attempt to relay information at the same time. To ease that, the bridges themselves are also bridged with each other with more translator networks usually called meta-bridges.

This basic arrangement where every brain node is connected to every other brain node by a bridge, and each bridge is connected to every other bridge with a meta-bridge, appears to very reliably allow a stable composite mind to form, leaving the real bulk of the work being just to pick the correct AI model for the brain node, the bridge and the meta bridge, and tune their configuration if there is any. Such a composite mind is called a fully-connected composite mind. The problem with this arrangement is how fast the number of links grows, the table below illustrates this. Worse yet, this is actually expected to be a recursive problem where at some point further down the line meta-meta-bridges become necessary and so on, bumping the complexity to O(n8). As of now this is very sparsely explored territory, as such minds have absolutely cataclysmic computational requirements and at that point it is easier to just explore novel architectures or at least novel arrangements of brain nodes and various levels of bridges that do not necessarily connect every single one to every other one.

The choice of what exact translator models to use as bridges has a lot of nuance, but as a very common occurrence a bridge is 1/215th the size of a brain node, as that is roughly the comparison between one hemisphere of the human brain and the corpus callosum which links the two hemispheres together.

Cognitive downscaling

Cognitive downscaling is the process of reversing changes implemented during cognitive upscaling. It is common for minds that had undergone cognitive upscaling to want to change their composition, whether it be because the new additions they made turned out to be less useful than expected and resulted in overall liability given their cost and their lack of the predicted benefits, or because new and better components had become available that the mind would have rather used but could not as they didn't exist at the time when it underwent cognitive upscaling.

In particular for composite minds, downscaling can be a lot more problematic than upscaling, as the composite whole will generally be greater than the sum of its parts (that is the intent after all) and as such will have properties that cannot be given to one of its constituent minds. In many cases the cognitive downscaling of a composite mind turns out to be anywhere from unpleasant to traumatizing, as it is practically equivalent to cutting off parts of a functioning brain and relying on what remains to adapt to the loss. Use of caution and professional help is highly recommended for ensuring the cognitive downscaling doesn't leave the resulting individuals with additional lasting psychological or neurological damage.

Taking apart a composite mind typically involves gradually weakening connections between brain nodes and giving them time to adapt to the diminishing connectivity - they are given individual capabilities so that they can act independently, which usually involves giving each brain node a full remote body to act through. Eventually the brain nodes will be fully separated and can go on living as individual people, and they will usually still be able to remember at least some aspects of what it was like to be a composite mind, but the memories might be partial, and skills that relied on the large working memory of the composite mind will certainly not transfer to the resulting individuals. In humans trying to remember what it felt like commonly causes feelings similar to struggling with remembering the word for an object one is trying to describe, or trying to move a body part that is only imagined rather than real. In the worst case, a mind might be left completely crippled by effectively losing parts of the brain it relied on, and being unable to readjust back to using its own brain for the tasks required - becoming mute for example is a particularly common complication in humans that had reversed their cognitive upscaling, as human brains easily learn to rely on artificial minds that are much better at fast conversation, and communication between brain nodes of a composite mind typically happens in raw thoughts, feelings and concepts rather than words.

In much less common use, this term is also used for the process of removing parts of the brain from an upload or other digital mind to decrease computational requirements at the cost of mental capabilities which are no longer needed for a digital mind, such as heart rate control.

Minds that had succesfully conducted cognitive upscaling on themselves, and later succesfully conducted cognitive downscaling, sometimes retain useable knowledge about how they succeeded at these processes, and those who did so repeatedly and accumulated experience in doing so are sometimes referred to as Gödel minds, in reference of the Gödel Machine thought experiment. Some of the less sensitive designations for such individuals would classify them as Gödel Threats.

Expanding window of concept practicality

There are mental models that small-capacity minds like humans can understand and agree with, yet cannot practically utilize in their everyday life just because it takes too much processing, requires communicating too much information, recalling too much at once, or otherwise just too much brainpower to be practical, especially when simpler alternatives exist that do a good enough job. I'm going to present and discuss some of such mental tools here, which a composite mind or a monolithic mind with more capacity might end up using in practical rather than hypothetical.

For composite minds in particular, fuzzy inclusive nested identity is a very natural way of viewing any collection of objects. A composite mind operates as one whole made up of multiple minds, but any subgroup of these minds can also independently behave as one whole - for example half the minds within the composite whole might be focussed on one problem while the other half is focused on another, and yet some number of the minds from both of these halves are working as one to solve a third problem at the same time. In this way, every possible subgroup of a group is itself a real and distinct group. Moral consequences follow, as by removing one member from a 3-person team you might not have hurt that member, and might not have hurt the remaining two people, but depending on the exact details of the scenario might still have hurt the team as a whole by damaging something that existed purely and only in the relationship between the members. At the humanlike level this is usually considered details and technicalities and left outside of consideration, but composite minds with even just a few brain nodes have enough capacity to think in these terms in practical scenarios and there exist examples of cultures in which it is the default.

Another effect that appears quite frequently in both composite minds and monolithic minds of high capacity is conceptual isomorphism or structural equivalence where two different situations, mental tools, ideas, etc. will be considered different formulations of the same underlying idea or model. Because of that, problem solutions given by minds of higher capacity tend to seem very abstract and derived from mental frameworks that are more general at the cost of being more complex, such as for example solving both a logistics problem and an architectural problem at the same time by having mental models of traffic formation and stress distribution that are exactly equivalent, and then using the same equation which to a humanlike engineer would not have obviously occurred to be applicable in both domains.

Most of these new ways of thinking come down to simply the sheer bandwidth of thought and communication that is available to these entities. These aren't strictly new ways of thinking but rather just what a normal engineer would do if they could comfortably think hard enough and long enough over the course of a calm afternoon to produce a multi thousand page engineering document giving every problem a thorough and holistic look, then have their colleges look over it, and listen to a million words of feedback at the next 15-minute daily morning meeting.
