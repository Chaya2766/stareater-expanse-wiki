Panpsychism

Often mistaken due to name alone for some kind of mysticism, panpsychism in the modern times describes a belief that consciousness and the ability to have subjective experiences is an inherent and deeply rooted feature of information processing. That any system capable of reacting to its environment has some form of consciousness and subjective experience.

This belief goes hand in hand with the naturalist approach to human consciousness which rejects that there is anything beyond simple physics governing the human body and human brain, and imposes that physics alone sufficiently explain human consciousness. This is sometimes called "naturalist panpsychism" to distinguish it from other kinds of panpsychism that exist.

Well convinced believers that use this mental model tend to look on in horror at just what people of the previous centuries used to be perfectly fine with, treating many other beings as just things that can't feel, or even if then not worth consideration anyway. Those with more cynical tendencies point out that the emergence of superintelligent entities likely posed a great pressure to adopt this view, as viewing even an ant's needs as meaningful does carry some benefit when someday there may be, as the common comparison goes, something so fast that to them you are just as much merely an ant, unless you want to imply that the cutoff of beings worth treating well is just below you, and then watch in horror as your superintelligent administrators decide that actually the cutoff is just below them and you're way too insignificant to consider.

Connection to naturalism

Naturalism denotes the belief that the nature of human consciousness is physical, that no souls or other supernatural causes exist to provide consciousness but, rather that merely the neurological activity of the human brain is entirely sufficient to produce it, or rather, the neurological activity of the human brain is the consciousness.

It is often actually said to be merely an extension of that idea, that if one accepts that the nature of consciousness is entirely physical, then anyone who believes so will arrive at panpsychism by merely striking down two additional assumptions:
  1. That consciousness is binary (either is or isn't), instead treating it as something that there can be varrying amounts of - eg. that both a dog and a human are conscious but perhaps the dog is less conscious, and a snail would be even less conscious than that but still conscious to some degree.

  2. That there is some lower limit below which consciousness cannot exist - if this assumption is taken as false together with the previous one, then consciousness is a smooth spectrum, which is present less and less in simpler organisms but never quite reaches zero, so even bacteria may have some absolutely miniscule and barely significant but still present amount of consciousness.
In practice assumption two still exists to some degree in modern panpsychists, for example a rock that cracks in half as a response to being struck did in a way process information, but can it really be thought of as it having "experienced" something in that brief flash of information processing? An absolute panpsychist would say yes, but many would not go that far, and some may opt to instead dodge the question by saying that "it does not matter in practice".

Downstream assumptions

Accepting that everything is conscious to some degree has a common tendency to produce odd implications depending on other beliefs of the person reasoning about it, especially if they previously held a more narrow idea of consciousness and have some pre-existing associations regarding the behavior of entities they believed to be conscious.

Fallacy 1: all things are intelligent to some degree - Intelligence and consciousness are two different and separate properties, and being conscious does not inherently make you intelligent. As such, even though under panpsychist belief nearly everything is conscious at least to some minimal degree, not everything is intelligent.

Fallacy 2: all things are capable of suffering - Suffering is a very complex behaviour crafted by evolution for the purpose of avoiding dangers and especially for learning. Most things are evolved but not capable of learning and so may have never had the need to develop the capacity for suffering. Additionally artificially created minds may have never needed to avoid dangers in the same way, or the capacity for suffering may have been artificially removed. As such even though in principle everything is conscious, not everything is capable of suffering.

Fallacy 3: all things have emotions - Emotions are just one specific type of subjective experience. Many people think of emotions as just something to produce correct behaviour over the medium long term such as hiding when a threat is present, which fill the gap between the immediate but short-lasting instinctive responses to sensations and the slow but relatively permanent effects of reasoning about information. As such, things that are conscious need not necessarily have had the pressure to develop sophisticated mechanisms like emotions.
An even deeper version of this fallacy is that all things are capable of the same emotions as humans, such as for example fear or lonliness. Those are also very specific mechanisms that had to evolve to fulfill specific needs, and as such may not be present in other conscious things, and other things may experience emotions that humans do not have. There is also no reason that experiences necessarily need to be some form of a sensation, an emotion or an awareness, but may also be something entirely different that does not occur within the human mind.

Reasons to believe

Panpsychic statements about the universality of consciousness and subjective experience are infamously unfalsifiable, nor are they provable. Indeed for any given person even the mere presence of other minds is unprovable, leading to the counterposition known as Solipsism, the belief that the self is the only thing that can be known and verified, or at more extreme, that the self is the only reality and no other minds truly exist.

The argument for panpsychism is moral in nature, and typically supported by posing two possibilities:
1. we adhere to panpsychism, and panpsychism turns out to be wrong - in this event we had been wasting time and resources considering the needs of lesser beings which in reality had no needs at all, and could never have suffered in the first place. This is inconvenient.
2. we reject panpsychism, and panpsychism turns out to be correct - in this event we had justified neglecting the needs of lesser beings and potentially exploiting them as mere resources while in reality they had experienced all of it. This creates suffering on astronomical scale.
Given the vast wealth of modern society it is considered not a big cost to extend moral consideration to beings far below the human level of complexity. In practice there is still some cutoff level where people will say that, for example bacteria may have minimal consciousness, but it is so minimal and the effort needed to account for them so great that it is simply not worth it. Individuals and cultures vary in where exactly that cutoff is placed, and indeed examples are known of that cutoff being placed above the human level of complexity such that only highly sophisticated superintelligences are seen as worth catering to.

On the other hand, the logical support for panpsychism relies largely on the fact that saying only humans or beings with humanlike complexity are conscious is a package of two extra arbitrary assumptions that needlessly complicate the concept of consciousness. This may be more or less convincing to any given individual or culture depending on their existing beliefs and mental models.
