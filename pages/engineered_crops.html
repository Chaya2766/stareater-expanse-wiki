Engineered crops

Growing plants remains a common method to produce food and other resources. Many crops had been engineered (genetically and otherwise) to have more desirable characteristics. This article lists just some of such cases of engineered plants as notable examples.

Kilotato

A genetically engineered descendant of the potato, named after its tendency to continue growing to a nearly unrestricted size. The kilotato was created primarily as a means to save the labor of automated systems by continuing to grow regardless of how long it is left unharvested, as such vastly decreasing the minimum amount of equipment necessary to harvest a field in time - whereas normally potatoes are meant to be harvested within 10 megaseconds (116 terran days) after being planted, the kilotatoes could be left in the soil for years and simply keep accumulating nutrients while a vastly underequipped team attempts to harvest the field, thus preventing the usual efficiency losses that come with having insufficient equipment for quick harvesting leaving plants laying dormant in the field. They also grow at roughly 10 times the rate as their unmodified ancestors, leading to them reaching a mass of 1 to 2 kilograms after the usual 10-megasecond growth cycle, but often they are harvested after just one megasecond while they're at a convenient size and mass of around 150 grams.

This doesn't come without downsides, as incidents of kilotatoes having been left forgotten show they are easily capable of growing to sizes at which they require complicated excavation to extract from the ground. In one case of a habitat having been uninhabited for 9.5Gs (a bit over 300 years), the kilotatoes grown on the farming fields had reached an average of 1.5 tonnes in mass each and 1.36m in diameter, with some reaching over 2 tonnes, requiring the field to be completely renovated as the kilotatoes could not be extracted using regular farming equipment. Said incident caused a renewed interest among hobbyist gardeners competing to cultivate the biggest kilotato but it is generally thought that the real record size kilotato may still lay in some abandoned field waiting to be discovered.

Sagnison

Sagnison is an artificial descendant of the bell pepper as well as to some degree the watermelon. It grows as a short bush and produces fruit in the form of tough leathery pouches filled with thick, honey-like fluid meant to contain all the needed minerals, vitamin, protein, amino acids, fats and carbohydrates in roughly right proportions for a balanced homo terran diet. The weight of the fruit limits the plant to be grown in low gravity environments, most desirably around 0.19g level.

The fruit last effectively indefinite time in storage due to the protective shell, but might quickly become a genuine biohazard if somehow punctured and left unnoticed, as the internal fluid is a perfect environment for fungal and bacterial growth. The pouch itself is also technically consumable and rich in dietary fiber, however it is not common to eat it for the same reasons as the hard part of a coconut is not usually eaten.

Qipio

Qipios are modified peppers cultivated mainly for their highly variable taste rather than nutritional value. The fruit are smooth and teardrop-shaped with the pointy end hanging towards the ground, with colour varying based on growing conditions. Internally they are structured somewhat similarly to tomatoes, but with the gelatinous parts replaced by a smooth paste. Red qipios, the most common kind, are said to most often have a mild sourness to them reminiscent of lemons, and contain some capsaicin, making them mildly spicy, and most notably have a significant taste of umami. Their use is primarily as an addition to other foods, as when eaten directly the spice can easily accumulate and become overwhelming.

The fruit grow on bushes about 1m tall and require specific conditions known best to those who actually grow them, including particularly precise control over the temperature which can significantly change the taste and colour of the resulting fruit. The taste is generally very consistently related to the colour as for instance blue qipios are known to be particularly spicy, while those closer to chartreuse particularly strongly show their sour side. Particularly experienced gardeners had even been able to produce gradient qipios that mix two or more of the generally recognized taste profiles in different parts of the fruit.
