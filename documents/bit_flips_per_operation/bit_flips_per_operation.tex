	\section{Assumptions}
	
	Why NAND gates?
	
	1. My whole system of FPGA-based "computronium" relies on a paper that among other things estimated the energy to flip a nand gate made using a number of different computing forms (eg. standard CMOS, other electronic, spintronic, nanomagnet logic, etc.).
	
	2. NAND gates alone can be used to construct any circuit. When combined the right way they can approximate any other gate.

	$$ G = 5 + (n-1)\cdot9 = 9n - 4$$

		\item $n$ is the number of bits of the adder
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Multiplication (integer and fixed-point)}
	
	(big thanks to this video \href{https://www.youtube.com/embed/O34KquoMpT0}{https://www.youtube.com/embed/O34KquoMpT0})
	
	\medskip
	
	The multiplier type I'll be using is simple cascading multiplier that works something like this:
	
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{c | c | c | c | c | c | c |}
			number A 	& & & & $A_2$ & $A_1$ & $A_0$ \\
			number B 	& & & & $B_2$ & $B_1$ & $B_0$ \\\hline
			partial 1	& & & & $A_2B_0$ & $A_1B_0$ & $A_0B_0$ \\
			partial 2	& & & $A_2B_1$ & $A_1B_1$ & $A_0B_1$ & \\
			partial 3	& & $A_2B_2$ & $A_1B_2$ & $A_0B_2$ & & \\\hline
			sum			& $P_5$ & $P_4$ & $P_3$ & $P_2$ & $P_1$ & $P_0$ \\
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	
	$A_n$ represents the nth bit of number A, and the same for number B.
	
	$P_n$ represents the nth bit of the result.
	
	\medskip
	
	Multiplying two bits (eg. $A_0$ with $B_0$ which would be written as just $A_0B_0$) is an AND operation. Every combination of two bits requires an AND gate, which is made from 2 NAND gates. This part adds $2n^2$ NAND gates to the multiplying circuit.
	
	\medskip
	
	The additions have to be done with adders such as shown in section \ref{addition}.
	
	In an n-bit multiplier each row will have length n and there will be n rows
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item First row (partial 1): does not require adders.
		
		(0)
		
		\item Second row (partial 2): requires a half-adder at the start, a half-adder at the end, and full adders through the middle.
		
		(2 half-adders and n-2 full adders, which is $2\cdot5 + (n-2)\cdot9 = 9n - 8$ NAND gates)
		
		\item Third row (partial 3): requires a half-adder at the start, and full adders the rest of the way.
		
		(1 half adder and n-1 full adders, which is $1\cdot5 + (n-1)\cdot9 = 9n-4$ NAND gates)
		
		\item Every row after the third is the same as the third, so the third row is additionally multiplied by n-2
	\end{itemize}
	
	In total we need to sum up these terms:
	
	$2n^2$ from the ANDs across the whole thing.
	
	$9n - 8$ from the adders in the second row.
	
	$(n-2)\cdot(9n-4) = (9n^2-22n+8)$ from the adders from row 3 onwards.
	
	\bigskip
	
	In total an n-bit multiplier should have this many gates:
	
	$$ G = 11n^2 - 13n $$
	
	where:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $G$ is the number of NAND gates in the multiplier
		
		\item $n$ is the number of bits of the adder
	\end{itemize}

	\section{Final gate count}
	
	I've also included gate count for a "multiply and accumulate" circuit, which is simply a multiplication combined with an addition, and I assume the needed circuit has as many gates as those two circuits added together.
	
	$$ G_{+/-} = 9n - 4 $$
	
	$$ G_{\times} = 11n^2 - 13n $$
	
	$$ G_{\div} = ??? $$
	
	$$ G_{+\times} = G_{+/-} + G_{\times} = 11n^2 - 4n - 4$$
	
	where:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $G_{+/-}$ is the number of NAND gates in an n-bit adder
		
		\item $G_{\times}$ is the number of NAND gates in an n-bit multiplier
		
		\item $G_{\div}$ is the number of NAND gates in an n-bit divider
		
		\item $G_{+\times}$ is the number of NAND gates in an n-bit multiply-and-accumulate circuit
		
		\item $n$ is the number of bits in an input variable
	\end{itemize}
	
	There exist optimizations that allow you to get gate counts smaller than that given by these equations, but they haven't been accounted for. These equations give you what you'll get if you just systematically apply the usual method of building these circuits with no additional tweaking.
